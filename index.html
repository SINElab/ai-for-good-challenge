Welcome to the AI for Good Challenge!

Artificial intelligence has the power to transform how we live, work, and connect. But technology creates its greatest value when it helps solve real human problems.

Led by the University at Albany AI & Society College, and funded by the SUNY AI for the Public Good Initiative, the AI for Good Challenge brings together students and faculty across four SUNY campuses (University at Albany, SUNY Oneonta, SUNY Cobleskill, and Hudson Valley Community College) to design AI-enabled solutions that address one of the most pressing social issues of our time: social isolation and loneliness. Participants will work in interdisciplinary teams to explore how AI can help reconnect people with their communities, strengthen social support networks, and improve access to meaningful opportunities for engagement.

Over two days, teams will move from problem discovery and human-centered design to prototype development, responsible AI assessment, and solution pitching. Along the way, participants will apply technical skills, creativity, ethical reasoning, and collaborative problem-solving to develop solutions that have the potential to create measurable social impact.

This Year’s Design Challenge

Rates of social isolation and loneliness have increased across age groups and communities. In Upstate New York, these challenges are often intensified by geographic dispersion, aging populations, limited transportation options, shrinking community institutions, and barriers to accessing local resources.

How might we use AI to reconnect socially isolated individuals with the people, places, organizations, and services that can strengthen their sense of belonging and participation in community life?

Participants are challenged to design solutions that:

  • Understand users through a simple, low-friction intake process.
  • Identify barriers to social connection and community participation.
  • Provide personalized recommendations and pathways to engagement.
  • Encourage sustained involvement with community networks and services.
  • Use AI to facilitate human relationships rather than substitute for them.

More Than a Hackathon

This challenge is not simply about building technology. It is about designing responsible, inclusive, and practical solutions that address real community needs. Successful teams will demonstrate how AI can:

  • Strengthen real-world human connections
  • Improve accessibility and inclusion
  • Support rural and underserved populations
  • Promote trust, privacy, and responsible innovation
  • Create meaningful and sustainable social impact

Join us as we explore how technology can help build stronger, more connected communities. Together, we can imagine and create AI solutions that put people first.

Schedule and event experience

The AI for Good Challenge is structured as a two-day, problem-first, faculty-mentored design sprint. Rather than emphasizing coding alone, the event should guide interdisciplinary student and faculty teams across campuses through a sequence of activities that includes:

  • Understanding a real community problem
  • Identifying affected stakeholders and community needs
  • Developing a focused and feasible AI-supported solution
  • Examining the solution’s ethical and social implications
  • Creating a prototype or proof of concept
  • Communicating the solution to judges and community partners
  • Reflecting on the learning process and possibilities for future development

Day 1: Understanding the Problem and Designing Solutions

Day 1 Objective

The goal for Day 1 is to understand the problem, define the user, identify barriers to connection, and design an AI-enabled solution that strengthens participation in community life. The main question teams will work on is: Who is experiencing social isolation, why is it happening, and how can AI help reconnect them to community life?

By the end of Day 1, teams should have:

  • A clearly defined target population
  • A well-articulated problem statement
  • An understanding of barriers to connection
  • A proposed solution concept
  • A user journey
  • A clear explanation of how AI adds value
  • A clear pathway from AI interaction to human connection

Expected output for Day 1 is a validated solution concept, not a prototype. In other words, teams should end Day 1 knowing: “This is the problem we are solving, for these users, and this is how our AI-based solution creates meaningful community connections”.

Day 2: Prototype Development and Validation

Day 2 Objective

The goal for Day 2 is to develop a prototype, evaluate its impact and responsibility, and communicate a compelling vision for implementation and scale. The main question teams will work on is: Can we demonstrate that this idea is feasible, responsible, and capable of creating real-world impact?

By the end of Day 2, teams should have:

  • A working software prototype (for example, a lightweight web application, mobile app, dashboard, or chatbot) that demonstrates the core functionality of the proposed solution
  • A UI/UX wireframe or user journey map illustrating the intended user experience, key workflows, and user interactions.
  • A pitch deck (5 to 10 slides) describing the problem, proposed solution, AI methodology, anticipated benefits, target users, implementation strategy, and expected societal value.
  • A responsible AI assessment addressing ethics, bias, fairness, transparency, privacy, security, potential risks, unintended consequences, and mitigation strategies.

Expected output for Day 2 is a prototype-supported proposal that demonstrates community need and relevance; social impact, equity, and accessibility; responsible use of AI, privacy and trust; and feasibility and sustainability.

There will be four submissions during the two days:

Day 1 - Milestone finalization point 2Teams submit solution overview, user journey map, description of AI functionality, and human connection strategy.

Day 2 - Milestone finalization point 4Teams submit final package consisting of working software prototype, UI/UX wireframe or user journey map, pitch deck, and responsible AI assessment.

Submissions will be collected through Microsoft Forms for standard file formats (e.g., pitch deck and responsible AI assessment). Code repositories, prototypes, and other materials may be submitted via a cloud storage service such as OneDrive. Submissions will automatically receive a timestamp. Late submissions could remain eligible for participation and feedback but become ineligible for competitive awards unless the delay resulted from a documented event-wide technical issue.

Judging criteria

Submissions will be evaluated on the following six dimensions:

25%Community need and relevance
20%Social impact, equity, and accessibility
15%AI appropriateness and innovation
20%Responsible AI, privacy, and trust
10%Feasibility and sustainability
10%Prototype, user experience and communication

Under each dimension, judges will consider the following:

Community need and relevance (25%)

  • Does the team understand the problem?
  • Does the solution drive meaningful in-person connection?
  • Does it address the needs of isolated individuals?
  • Is the solution designed for the realities of the region and local communities?

Social impact, equity, and accessibility (20%)

  • Will the solution generate meaningful social benefit?
  • Does it reach seniors, rural residents, low-digital-literacy populations, and non-smartphone users?
  • Is accessibility built into the design, including consideration of ADA and WCAG accessibility principles, where applicable?
  • Does it promote inclusion and equity?

AI appropriateness and innovation (15%)

  • Is AI being used where it adds genuine value?
  • Is the approach creative and innovative?
  • Does the solution make meaningful use of contextual information such as language, culture, age, or user needs?

Responsible AI, privacy, and trust (20%)

  • Does the solution respect privacy and dignity?
  • Are consent and transparency addressed?
  • Have risks, biases, and unintended consequences been identified?
  • Are mitigation strategies realistic and appropriate?

Feasibility and sustainability (10%)

  • Can the solution realistically be implemented?
  • Will it work in rural and small-town settings?
  • Can it be maintained and scaled over time?

Prototype, user experience, and communication (10%)

  • Does the prototype effectively demonstrate the concept?
  • Is the user journey clear and intuitive?
  • Does the pitch communicate the idea convincingly?

General Information

What is the SUNY for the Public Good initiative?

It is a partnership between the University at Albany, SUNY Oneonta, SUNY Cobleskill, and Hudson Valley Community College to strengthen AI teaching and research across the region. It includes four coordinated initiatives:

  • The AI Preparedness Academy and Faculty Learning Community, a collaborative initiative designed to build institutional capacity for AI by equipping faculty with the tools and critical frameworks to teach about and with AI, while fostering interdisciplinary dialogue, hands-on learning and exchange of practices.
  • The AI for Good Challenge, an annual cross-campus competition bringing together interdisciplinary teams of students and faculty to develop AI-powered solutions to real-world community challenges.
  • The Visiting/Affiliated Faculty Program, a collaborative effort supporting faculty in co-developing AI-infused microcredentials, courses and instructional materials, expanding interdisciplinary learning opportunities for students across institutions.
  • The AI Plus Collaborative Research Experience (CORE), a hands-on research and mentorship program connecting undergraduate students, graduate assistants and faculty in cross-campus teams, culminating in a Summer Research Intensive hosted by UAlbany.
What is the AI for Good Challenge?

The AI for Good Challenge is a two-day innovation competition that brings together students and faculty from diverse disciplines to design AI-enabled solutions that address a significant social challenge. This year's focus is social isolation and loneliness, with an emphasis on strengthening community connections and improving participation in community life.

What is this year's design challenge?

Participants are asked to address the following question: How might we use AI to reconnect socially isolated individuals with the people, places, organizations, and services that can strengthen their sense of belonging and participation in community life?

Who can participate?

The challenge is open to both undergraduate and graduate students as well as faculty from all academic disciplines. Teams with technical, design, social science, public policy, health, business, and other backgrounds are encouraged to participate.

Do I need programming experience?

No. Technical expertise is not required. Teams are encouraged to combine diverse skills and perspectives. Strong ideas, user-centered design, accessibility, responsible AI practices, and community impact are valued as highly as technical implementation.

Can I participate if I have never used AI tools before?

Yes. The challenge is designed to be accessible to participants with varying levels of experience. Resources and learning materials will be provided to help participants get started.

Technology and Development

Do we have to build a fully functional application?

No. Teams are expected to develop a proof of concept that demonstrates the key ideas behind their solution. Judges will evaluate both the concept and its implementation.

What technologies can we use?

Teams may use any appropriate tools, including:

  • Generative AI tools
  • Low-code and no-code platforms
  • Design and prototyping tools
  • Open-source software
  • Machine learning frameworks
  • Public datasets
  • Cloud services and APIs
  • Commercial tools with appropriate licenses
Can we use generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, or Gemini?

Yes. Participants are encouraged to use AI tools responsibly and transparently as part of their development process.

Can we use pre-existing code or libraries?

Yes. Teams may use publicly available libraries, frameworks, and tools, provided they comply with applicable licensing requirements and properly acknowledge their use.

Judging

How will projects be evaluated?

Projects will be judged on:

  • Community need and relevance
  • Social impact, equity, and accessibility
  • AI appropriateness and innovation
  • Responsible AI, privacy, and trust
  • Feasibility and sustainability
  • Prototype, user experience, and communication
Is the most technically advanced project likely to win?

Not necessarily. Judges will evaluate how well solutions address the challenge, create social value, promote accessibility and inclusion, and use AI appropriately. Technical sophistication is only one component of evaluation.

Are accessibility and inclusion important?

Yes. Solutions should consider the needs of rural communities, aging populations, individuals with limited digital literacy, and people from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds.

What does "AI should facilitate human relationships rather than substitute for them" mean?

The goal is to design solutions that help people connect with other people, organizations, services, and community activities. Successful solutions will demonstrate how AI supports real-world engagement rather than replacing it.
